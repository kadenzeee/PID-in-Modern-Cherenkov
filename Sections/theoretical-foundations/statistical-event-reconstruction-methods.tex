% Content for the "Statistical Event Reconstruction Methods" subsection

The problem of event reconstruction in modern Cherenkov detectors is to determine what happened inside the
detector based on the data obtained from the photon sensors. The most common 
current way of doing this is through reverse ray-tracing. An algorithm
iterates through the possible paths a photon can take through the detector,
and evaluates a Gaussian likelihood under the hypothesis of each particle type \nolinebreak\cite{Santelj_2017}.
This likelihood is built from the spatial and temporal residuals between the
detected photon hits and the expected photon hits from the ray-tracing. For example, a Gaussian spatial
likelihood can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_h = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma} \exp \left( -{\frac{( x_i - \langle x_{i,h} \rangle)^2}{2 \sigma^2}} \right)
\end{equation}
where \(x_i\) is the detected position of the \(i\)-th photon, \(\langle x_{i,h} \rangle\) is the expected position
of the \(i\)-th photon under hypothesis \(h\), and \(\sigma\) is the spatial resolution of the detector system.
The hypothesis \(h\) can be for example an incident pion or a kaon. Note that many algorithms choose to use non-Gaussian likelihoods. 
By calculating the likelihood for each hypothesis, we can then form a log-likelihood ratio to determine which 
hypothesis is more probable:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \log \mathcal{L} = \log \mathcal{L_{\text{kaon}} - \log \mathcal{L}_{\text{pion}}}
\end{equation}
This can be minimised across all detector hits to find the most probable particle type using various
numerical methods. Other likelihood-based methods include time-imaging \nolinebreak\cite{time-imaging-roman_2020}, 
where precise photon timing information is also used to build probability density functions (PDFs) as a supplement to spatial PDFs 
for each particle hypothesis. The detected photon times are then compared to these PDFs to form likelihoods. Many modern algorithms
use a combination of spatial, temporal, angular information from other sub-detectors, and even photon count information to build more robust likelihood functions.

Likelihood-based methods are among the most powerful and widely used analysis techniques in countless experiments. Their practical 
application, however, often requires detailed construction and evaluation of high-dimensional likelihoods, 
leading to significant computational overhead in complex detectors.
Machine learning (ML) approaches offer a promising alternative by replacing explicit likelihood construction with data-driven inference. 
Neural networks can be trained to learn the detector response to different particle hypotheses directly, thereby implicitly capturing the 
information normally encoded in likelihood functions. This has 
already seen encouraging results on Geant4-simulated data for the Hyper-Kaminokande
experiment \nolinebreak\cite{Prouse_2023} and for the PANDA Barrel DIRC \nolinebreak\cite{film-networks-markhoff_2026}.
