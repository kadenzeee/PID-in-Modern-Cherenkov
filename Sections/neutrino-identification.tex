% Content for the "Topological Detectors for Neutrino Astrophysics" subsection

Topological Cherenkov detectors function more as particle trackers than devices optimised for particle-species discrimination. They image Cherenkov photons across large distances of particle tracks, measuring interaction topology, particle direction, and energy, rather than single-track observables such as the Cherenkov angle \(\theta_c\). Since neutrino interactions are rare and large in scale, the detector and radiator material must span an enormous volume to obtain a sufficient flux. The simple solution to this is to use large natural volumes of material such as water or ice. This presents a unique set of engineering challenges, such as waterproofing the detectors and synchronising timing across large distances. It is also very difficult to separate the weakly interacting neutrino events from other background. Different collaborations have taken unique approaches to these problems depending on their physics programmes.

\subsection{Sources of Neutrinos}

There are many astrophysical sources of neutrinos of current interest, such as active galactic nuclei, neutron stars, gamma-ray bursts, and supernovae, ranging from MeV to PeV energies. These neutrinos are predominantly products of pion, muon and neutron decays. 
\begin{align*}
\pi^+ \rightarrow \mu^+ + \nu_{\mu} \\ 
\mu^+ \rightarrow e^+ + \nu_e + \bar{\nu}_{\mu} \\
n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
\end{align*}

The relative contribution of these channels depends on the production environment and neutrino energy. There are also ultra-high energy sources of neutrinos, ranging from PeV to EeV energies. Protons near black holes are accelerated to incredible velocities where they are able to interact with the cosmic microwave background. Then, the produced pions decay into extremely fast-travelling neutrinos. This process is known as the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin (GZK) process, as depicted in Fig \ref{fig:cosmic-neutrinos}.

Detectors investigating these processes must use kiloton-gigaton volumes of radiator material to track these neutrinos due to their high energies and minimal cross-section. As such, the material used must be cost effective and easy to handle.

\subsection{Water Cherenkov Detectors (Super-K)\label{sec:super-k}}
Water Cherenkov Detectors (WCDs) use large volumes of ultra-pure water as the radiator material. The Super-Kamiokande (Super-K)
experiment in Japan is a famous example of a WCD, using 50,000 tons of water surrounded by over 11,000 PMTs,
buried under a kilometre of mountain to shield from natural radiation. The detector is used to study neutrinos at MeV-GeV 
energies from various sources, including the sun, atmosphere, supernovae. The detector has investigated neutrino-specific phenomena, which before Super-K were largely unexplored.\nolinebreak\cite{super-k-neutrino-osc_1998}

Super-K is a precision detector for low energy neutrino physics. If we wish to study higher energy phenomena, such as neutrinos generated by the GZK process, we need to significantly increase the scale of the detector. This means that using pure water is unfeasible, and we must search for natural features on the Earth to act as a Cherenkov radiator.

\subsection{Ice Cherenkov Detectors (IceCube)}

The IceCube Neutrino Observatory is a cubic kilometre scale Cherenkov detector buried deep in the natural Antarctic ice sheet.\nolinebreak\cite{Aartsen_2017} 
It uses over 5,000 photodetector modules arranged on 86 strings, deployed between 1.5 and 2.5 km deep. The intra-string separation varies from 41 - 105m. The detector is designed to study ultra-high energy neutrinos. These neutrinos are filtered from low energy neutrinos and other background by using the entire Earth as a shield; up-going events are strongly enriched in high-energy neutrino interactions, as atmospheric muons are absorbed by the Earth. 

Fig \nolinebreak\ref{fig:ice-cube-event-display} shows such a cosmic event from IceCube, where a high energy neutrino has interacted with the ice to produce muons and subsequent Cherenkov radiation.
The thickness of the strings represents photon intensity, while the colours represent timing information, red being at the beginning of an event and green/blue being later in time.
