% Content for the "SiPMs" subsection

Silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) have been gaining traction recently due to 
developments in materials, leading to a great ease of manufacturing. They are
tiny compared to an MCP-PMT, due to their use of single photon avalanche diodes (SPADs), each containing
an avalanche photodiode and a quenching resistor. 
The simulations by F. Acerbi and S. Gundacker\nolinebreak\cite{ACERBI201916} 
provide an excellent in depth description of SiPMs.

SiPMs are much cheaper than MCP-PMTs and have a higher QE, however suffer from a high dark count rate and do not necessarily have similar hit rate capabilities, as the hit rate is mainly dependent on the density of SPADs. This
has led to the explosion of SiPMs in fields like positron emission photography (PET)
where precise photon timings are not necessary, and where ruggedness and sturdiness
is required. SiPMs also operate at a far lower voltage than MCP-PMTs and are not affected
by magnetic fields, adding to their dependability in medical settings.

The high dark count rate degrades PID in extremely precise systems, as noise inherently causes statistical
event reconstruction methods to struggle. Also, the dead time of SiPMs poses thought-provoking problems for DIRC systems
especially, which rely on photon arrival time for strong PID performance. In DIRC systems a few thousand photons 
are guided to a small detector area and all arrive typically within around 20-50ns, depending on the length of the 
detector. The photon multiplicity of a small area on the photosensor array can often be >10. The typical dead time of one SPAD is between 10–50ns \nolinebreak\cite{Bruschini2019},
so even though the microcells are much smaller than an MCP-PMT pixel, in the particularly photon-dense areas of the DIRC, it is possible to entirely lose some photons due to the dead time of the SiPM.

SiPMs provide an interesting alternative to MCP-PMTs, where their low cost and robust 
characteristics make them an extremely lucrative option for many applications in photon imaging. Their 
performance in high-energy physics applications is certainly going to improve in the future, and 
they are already a serious consideration for future LHCb RICH upgrades \nolinebreak\cite{WOTTON2024168824}
despite being a relatively new technology (circa 2000) compared to PMTs (1930) \nolinebreak\cite{LUBSANDORZHIEV2006236}.
